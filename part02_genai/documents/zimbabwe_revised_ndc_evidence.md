# Zimbabwe Revised Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), 2021 — structured evidence extract

This file contains structured extracts from passages supplied by the user. It is not a substitute for the complete original NDC PDF. Printed page numbers below refer to the page numbers printed in the NDC, not PDF viewer page indexes.

## Printed page 3
Zimbabwe's economy relies on agriculture and forestry, which employ 70% of the population directly or indirectly.

## Printed page 6
Zimbabwe's climate vulnerability is associated with factors including insufficient water, poverty, heavy reliance on rain-fed agriculture and natural resources, high population growth, gender vulnerabilities, weak early-warning systems and low awareness.

## Printed page 9
Climate-related water supply impacts include water shedding in Bulawayo. The NDC estimates a 38% decline in water availability per capita by 2050. The document also discusses impacts on tourism and mining.

## Printed page 10
Natural capital impacts: The 1991/1992 drought, described by the NDC as the most extreme drought in the past three decades, reduced Victoria Falls water discharge and plant biodiversity in the rainforest. It resulted in the deaths of about 148 elephants in Hwange National Park and about 1,500 elephants in Gonarezhou National Park.

## Printed page 11
Natural capital — fall armyworm: The fall armyworm invasion in 2016 caused estimated maize yield losses of 263,000 tons, valued at about US$83 million.

Natural capital — livestock: Low rainfall conditions, for example during the 2015–2016 drought, increased livestock disease rates. Livestock disease accounted for 61% of reported cattle deaths, particularly in Manicaland and Masvingo provinces.

Natural capital — cattle disease: Between November 2017 and May 2018, a combination of late heavy rains and lack of cattle dipping led to over 3,000 cattle dying from tick-borne diseases, primarily Theileriosis. Do not attribute this statistic to drought alone.

Financial capital — crop losses: The estimated value of crop losses each year is US$126 million. Estimated crop losses were US$321 million in 2001 and US$513 million in 2008.

Financial capital — prices and household assets: During droughts, family savings decrease. Product prices increase during drought years by 30–40%. During the 2015–2016 drought, cattle prices in high-rainfall areas ranged from US$350–450, while communities in drought-affected districts sold cattle for as little as US$20–60 to buy food.

Financial capital — industry and employment: Reduced supplies of agricultural raw materials reduce employment in industry and commerce. During the 1991/92 extreme drought, a number of industries faced closure, particularly in Bulawayo.

## Printed page 12
The NDC describes projected climate changes, including an expected decrease in rainfall of approximately 10%, and regional differences. These are projections, not observed historical changes.

## Printed page 20
The revised NDC mitigation analysis, using the Zimbabwean LEAP model, estimates that total GHG emissions in 2030 could be reduced to 44.7 MtCO2e in the mitigation scenario. Figure 6 presents a 40% reduction in per-capita GHG emissions below business-as-usual (BAU) by 2030. The population and GDP projections are based on NDS1 assumptions, including the impact of COVID-19 and expected economic recovery.

## Printed page 21
Table 4 reports 2017 GHG emissions, 2030 baseline emissions and 2030 emissions with mitigation measures. The total 2030 baseline is 75.39 million tonnes CO2-equivalent; the mitigation scenario total is 44.74 million tonnes CO2-equivalent, a reported reduction of 40.7%. Sector reductions relative to the baseline are: Energy 4.20 MtCO2e; IPPU 0.45 MtCO2e; Agriculture, Forestry and Other Land Use (AFOLU) 25.35 MtCO2e; Waste 0.65 MtCO2e. AFOLU contributes the largest sectoral reduction. Zimbabwe's 2017 share of global emissions is reported as 0.07%.

## Printed page 22
The mitigation contribution is conditional on appropriate international support. The estimated total investment cost is approximately US$4,834.47 million. The NDC notes that not all of this represents new additional cost and that cost estimates do not account for all expected benefits and co-benefits. Implementation is subject to affordable international financial support, investment, ability to leverage domestic resources, technology transfer and capacity development.

## Printed page 23
The NDC identifies co-benefits of mitigation measures, including improved air quality and health, forest-related improvements in soil and groundwater quality and surface-runoff regulation, employment creation through composting and waste-to-energy, reduced pollution, improved energy supply and reduced reliance on climate-sensitive hydropower. The energy sector, including transport, accounted for 33% of total national GHG emissions in 2017. Within the energy sector, thermal power generation accounted for 37.71%, residential sources 19.08%, road transportation 15.48%, and agriculture 13.84%.

## Printed page 24
Table 5 lists energy-sector mitigation measures, including reduced transmission and distribution losses, solar expansion, microgrids, biogas capacity, energy-efficiency improvements, biodiesel blending, fuel-efficiency improvements and a shift from private cars to public transport. The NDC identifies lack of funding for large capital projects and the need to upgrade the electricity grid as implementation challenges.

## Printed page 25
Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU) contributed 1.2 MtCO2e (3.3%) to Zimbabwe's 2017 emissions, compared with 0.7 MtCO2e (2.2%) in 2010. The largest IPPU sources were ferrochrome production (37%), cement production (32%), HFC consumption (25%) and chemical production (6%). Table 6 lists mitigation measures including clinker substitution, a secondary catalyst for nitrous oxide emissions and an HFC phasedown schedule.

## Printed page 26
AFOLU accounted for 54% of Zimbabwe's GHG emissions in 2017. The sector changed from a net sink between 1990 and 1993 to an increasing net source from 1994 to 2017. Drivers include deforestation from agricultural expansion, fuelwood gathering, increased livestock, commercial logging, veld fires, harvesting construction timber, illegal settlements and mining, tobacco curing and charcoal making.

The NDC's selected AFOLU mitigation measures include increasing natural forest land from 9.9 million hectares to 10.4 million hectares by 2025; increasing forest plantation area from 68,848 hectares to 118,848 hectares by 2025; and reducing the area burned by 500,000 hectares between 2020 and 2025. The document notes that livestock and climate-smart agriculture measures were not included in this mitigation analysis because robust data or a clear local implementing agency was lacking.

## Printed page 27
The NDC's waste-sector section reports that GHG emissions from waste gradually increased from 1990, reaching 1.76 MtCO2e in 2017. The section discusses waste-to-energy projects in Bulawayo, Harare, Gweru and Mutare and long-term composting of 20% of organic matter. Co-benefits include employment creation, improved environmental aesthetics and local air quality, reduced pollution to soil and water bodies, and an alternative energy source that could reduce reliance on hydropower during drought-related electricity shortfalls.

## Printed pages 28–29
The NDC identifies national policies and strategies supporting implementation, including Vision 2030, the National Development Strategy 1 (2021–2025), National Climate Policy (2017), National Climate Change Response Strategy (2015), Low Emissions Development Strategy (2020–2050), Revised National Gender Policy (2017) and National Youth Policy (2013). It also refers to adaptation policies and plans including the NAP Roadmap, Green Climate Fund Country Programme, draft National Water Resources Master Plan (2020–2040), National Agriculture Policy Framework (2018–2030) and Zimbabwe Climate Smart Agriculture Investment Plan.

## Printed page 30
The Ministry responsible for climate change management coordinates NDC implementation and international reporting to the UNFCCC. The Ministry of Finance and Economic Development plays a central role in coordinating and financing climate-related projects and programmes. ZimStat and the Environmental Management Agency are identified as data providers for the NDC monitoring, reporting and verification system.

## Printed pages 31–34
The NDC describes the institutional and financial framework for implementation. The estimated cost of mitigation measures is US$4,834.47 million; adaptation measures are to be costed in the forthcoming National Adaptation Plan. Financing sources include domestic and international public and private finance, the National Climate Change Fund, the proposed Climate Finance Facility, the Green Climate Fund and the Adaptation Fund. The document notes that Zimbabwe does not yet have permanent institutional structures to track all development and climate finance and describes DEVPROMIS as a system being developed to improve transparency and accountability.

## Printed pages 35–44
The NDC's Information for Clarity, Transparency and Understanding (ICTU) section states that the 2030 target is a 40% reduction in GHG emissions per capita compared with BAU, conditional on international support. The implementation period is 1 January 2021 to 31 December 2030, with a single-year target for 2030. Sectors covered are Energy, Waste, Industrial Processes and Product Use (IPPU), and Agriculture, Forestry and Land Use (AFOLU). Gases covered include carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and hydrofluorocarbons. The document reports 2017 national emissions of 35.84 MtCO2e and projected 2030 BAU emissions of 75.39 MtCO2e. It describes the use of the 2006 IPCC Guidelines and the LEAP model for emissions accounting and mitigation analysis.
